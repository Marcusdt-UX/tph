Think about the last time you walked into a store you'd never been in before. You looked around for signs. Where's checkout? Where's the thing I came for? A good store makes that obvious in about three seconds. A bad one makes you wander until you give up and leave.

Your website menu does the exact same job. It's the map your visitors use to get around, and when we do web design in Port Huron, it's one of the first things we look at on a site that isn't pulling its weight. A confusing menu quietly costs you customers every single day, and most business owners never realize it's happening.

## Your Menu Is the First Thing People Actually Use

Visitors don't read your homepage top to bottom. They scan it, and then they look for a way to get where they want to go. That way is your navigation menu, usually sitting across the top of the page.

If someone lands on your site wanting to know what you charge, and there's no obvious "Pricing" or "Services" link, they don't email you to ask. They hit the back button and go to the next business in the search results. The menu isn't decoration. It's the difference between a visitor becoming a customer and a visitor becoming someone else's customer.

## Keep It Short and Say What You Mean

The most common mistake we see is menus that try to do too much. Ten links across the top, half of them clever names that mean nothing to an outsider. "Solutions." "The Experience." "Our World." Nobody knows what those lead to.

Aim for five to seven items, tops. Use plain words that match what people are actually looking for: Home, Services, Pricing, About, Contact. Boring? Maybe. But boring and clear beats clever and confusing every time. Your visitor isn't grading your creativity. They're trying to find something.

If you offer a lot of services, you don't need a link for each one in the top menu. Use a single "Services" link that opens to a page listing everything, or a simple dropdown. Cramming it all up top just buries the important stuff.

## Put the Important Things Where People Expect Them

There are patterns people have learned from using thousands of other websites, and fighting those patterns only creates friction. Your logo goes top left and links back to the home page. Your main menu runs across the top. Your "Contact" or a phone number sits at the top right where people look for it.

This is also where a lot of sites drop the ball on phones. A menu that looks fine on a desktop can turn into a mess on a small screen. Since most of your local traffic is coming from someone's phone, your menu needs to collapse into a clean tap-to-open button and still work with a thumb. We dig into why that matters in our post on [mobile-first design](/blog/mobile-first-design/), and it applies double to navigation.

## Make Every Page Reachable in Two Clicks

Here's a simple test. Pick any page on your site. Starting from the home page, can a visitor get there in two clicks or less? If it takes four or five, that page is basically hidden, and hidden pages don't earn you anything.

Flat and shallow beats deep and nested. Your most important pages, the ones that turn browsers into buyers, should be one click from anywhere on the site. That usually means a clear top menu plus a footer that repeats your key links and contact details.

## Don't Forget the Footer

People scroll to the bottom of a page when they're looking for something specific: your phone number, your address, your hours, a link to contact you. The footer is prime real estate, and an empty one is a wasted opportunity.

Put your business name, service area, phone, and a short list of your main links down there. For a Blue Water area business, spelling out that you serve Port Huron, Fort Gratiot, Marysville, and the rest of St. Clair County also helps your local SEO while it helps your visitor.

## Guide People Toward the Next Step

Good navigation doesn't just let people wander. It nudges them toward doing something. That "Contact" button in the corner, styled to stand out from the rest of the menu, gives every visitor an obvious next move no matter what page they're on.

A menu that leads nowhere is a common version of the [website mistakes that drive customers away](/blog/website-mistakes-that-drive-customers-away/). Every page should make the next step easy to find, and your navigation is doing a lot of that work whether you planned it or not.

## The Bottom Line

Your menu is the quietest hardest-working part of your website. Keep it short, use words people understand, put things where they expect them, and make sure it works on a phone. Do that, and you remove a whole layer of friction between a visitor showing up and a visitor reaching out.
